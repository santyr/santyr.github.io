# The Unreadable Language of Perception

*DMT, alien glyphs, repeating worlds, and the generative nature of consciousness*

*Sat Antyr · An exploratory essay and working hypothesis · October 2026*

## Working hypothesis

**The Constrained Generative Perception Hypothesis:** Conscious perception is not a direct copy of the world. It emerges from an ongoing interaction between sensory evidence, learned expectations, internally generated patterns, and the brain's models of objects, space, self, and other agents. Psychedelic states may alter how these influences are balanced, allowing normally implicit processes of visual and conceptual construction to become unusually apparent. Shared neural architecture may consequently produce similar geometric patterns, unreadable scripts, apparent object duplication, and experiences of communication across different people, without requiring a shared external language, hidden cosmic code, or independently existing entities.

This is a *working hypothesis*, not an established theory. The observations motivating it are my own recalled DMT experiences, not recordings or controlled measurements. The mechanisms proposed to explain them remain open to experimental testing. A useful explanation must distinguish **what is experienced** from **what can be established about its cause**.

## Summary

During DMT experiences, unfamiliar glyphs can appear to be physically embedded in surfaces such as woven fabric or skin. They can seem to move with an object and persist across changes in viewpoint. In other experiences, naturally distributed clouds or trees can appear to be exact duplicates, while rocks, sand, and unfamiliar plants can form repeating, tile-like patterns. As the altered state subsides, the apparent duplicates may gradually resolve into individually distinct objects. These observations suggest at least three separable phenomena: geometric pattern generation, repetition of complex object appearances, and the stabilization of generated details within perceptual models of surfaces and scenes.

The glyphs frequently *look* like writing without being readable. Their compelling sense of concealed meaning may arise when visual pattern recognition labels a configuration as symbolic while meaning-making systems supply the expectation of a message. Apparent telepathic communications—sometimes experienced as internal words, sometimes as immediate understanding, sometimes as complex visual forms—may involve additional processes of agency attribution and metacognition. Research on geometric hallucinations, vision, writing systems, and DMT provides relevant findings and models, but does not establish this complete chain of explanation [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3), [[4]](#ref-4), [[5]](#ref-5), [[6]](#ref-6), [[11]](#ref-11), [[12]](#ref-12), [[13]](#ref-13), [[14]](#ref-14).

The DMT laser experiment, Allyson Grey's *Secret Writing*, Diana Slattery's xenolinguistics research, and the purported Project Serpo glyphs offer useful comparisons [[7]](#ref-7), [[8]](#ref-8), [[9]](#ref-9), [[10]](#ref-10). None currently demonstrates an extraterrestrial language or underlying code of reality. Their value may instead be in showing how humans construct, recognize, and invest symbols with meaning. The larger philosophical implication is not that the external world is unreal, but that our *experience* of it is a highly sophisticated, usually reliable, and sometimes conspicuously generative representation.

## 1. Perception is an inference, not a photograph

In ordinary experience, the visual world appears continuous and self-evident. Yet the information reaching the eyes is incomplete and constantly changing. To recognize a moving hand as the same hand, maintain the identity of a tree while shifting viewpoint, or infer the form of an object partly hidden from view, the brain must integrate sensory signals with previously learned structure and expectations. Models of peripheral vision show how performance on visual tasks can be explained using compressed statistical representations, rather than complete inventories of every detail. This is evidence that such representations can be useful explanatory models, not proof that all conscious vision is synthesized in that way [[1]](#ref-1).

Predictive-processing accounts emphasize this interaction between sensory evidence and expectations. The REBUS model of psychedelic action suggests that some high-level constraints become less dominant during psychedelic states, permitting changes in perception and interpretation [[2]](#ref-2). It would be too simple to say that DMT merely strengthens expectations: different expectations and neural signals may change their influence at different levels. The more cautious proposal is that the *relationship* between incoming evidence and generated models becomes altered.

For our purposes, the useful picture is a feedback process rather than a one-way pipeline. Sensory evidence and learned structure jointly constrain an interpretation, whose expectations can then be compared with further evidence. The diagram sketches that idea without claiming to locate a particular neural circuit.

The interesting possibility is that altered states make components of this constructive process visible to introspection, although introspection alone cannot reveal which specific neurons or computations are responsible.

## 2. When an apparent glyph becomes part of an object

The most tangible example begins with something ordinary: the coarse woven fabric of my work pants. Its visible crossings provide a lattice on which unfamiliar glyphs appear. The symbols can seem to be printed or woven into the surface rather than floating over the visual field. When the fabric moves, the glyphs move with it; looking away and looking back can reveal what seems to be a persistent arrangement. Similar markings can appear on the skin of a hand and follow its contours as the hand rotates.

If the apparent symbols were simply momentary visual noise, their object-linked stability would be surprising. An alternative is that generated visual features become temporarily bound to the brain's representation of the underlying surface. The perceptual system already predicts how real markings should transform as an object bends, turns, or changes distance. If a generated marking is mistakenly treated as a property of that object, it could be carried along by the same machinery of perceptual stability.

This is an **object-anchoring hypothesis**, not proof that DMT creates persistent external inscriptions or that the brain stores a complete three-dimensional glyph map. It makes a more modest claim: perception may integrate internally generated features into its stable representation of an object, and reconstruct those features while conditions permit. The apparent persistence of a model until enough evidence forces its revision is central to this interpretation. However, a similar reconstruction on each glance could also feel like the same enduring inscription; retrospective confidence alone cannot distinguish those possibilities.

## 3. Two kinds of repetition: objects and textures

Surface-bound glyphs suggest stability within an object; the clouds and trees suggest repetition across objects. In these experiences, clouds or trees remained naturally distributed across the scene, yet their detailed forms seemed identical, as though a complex object template had been reused in different places. The perceived duplication did not require a regular grid. As the DMT effects diminished, the clouds gradually appeared to regain their ordinary individuality.

Other experiences involved the ground, where sand, rocks, and unfamiliar or nonexistent plants appeared to form repeated spatial arrangements. These had a more tile-like quality, similar to the reuse of small textures in computer graphics. The important distinction is between **repeating what an object looks like** and **repeating how visual elements are arranged in space**. Those may be different kinds of perceptual alteration, and they should not be assumed to have a single neural cause. Here, “identical” describes how the objects appeared to me; it is not a measured, detail-by-detail equivalence.

The *texture synthesis* analogy is useful because computational vision can represent complex surfaces through local statistical summaries. It is not evidence that neurons literally copy and paste cloud images or run a graphics engine. The testable idea is that DMT might alter how object identity, detailed appearance, and spatial arrangement are differentiated or reconstructed. Observations that apparently identical clouds gradually become ordinary distinct clouds as the effects wear off are particularly suggestive of shifting constraints on scene interpretation, but they do not identify the mechanism [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

## 4. Why geometry can look like an unreadable language

Repetition alone does not explain why some generated patterns look like a language. To approach that question, consider the elementary configurations used in visual recognition: straight and curved segments, corners, crossings, branches, closures, symmetries, and repeated intervals. These are not an innate alphabet. They are examples of the kinds of features from which many objects and symbols can be recognized. Bressloff and colleagues demonstrated how recurring geometric hallucination patterns can be related mathematically to aspects of visual cortical organization, including lattice-like structures and spirals [[3]](#ref-3).

Written language is much newer than the visual machinery used to recognize it. Dehaene and Cohen's *neuronal recycling* account proposes that reading recruits preexisting cortical systems [[4]](#ref-4). Changizi and colleagues found statistical regularities among the contour configurations of human writing systems and other visual signs, suggesting that scripts have been culturally shaped by features to which human vision is well adapted [[5]](#ref-5). Consequently, an invented collection of marks can look convincingly like writing even if it has no stable correspondence to sounds, words, or concepts.

The distinction between *recognizing writing* and *reading meaning* may be the heart of the phenomenon. A glyph can provoke the unmistakable impression that it belongs to a language while resisting translation. An altered state may intensify the sense that meaning is present and concealed, without providing any corresponding message. In that case, the mind is not necessarily perceiving an undeciphered language; it may be generating **the experience of encountering a language**. Unreadability alone cannot settle the issue, since a real but unfamiliar script is also unreadable to someone who has never learned it. What is missing is an independently demonstrated mapping between the marks and information.

A 2022 qualitative analysis of 3,778 published DMT experiences found that reports frequently mentioned geometric visual content, while entity encounters and communications formed another notable category [[6]](#ref-6). Such datasets illuminate what people report, but cannot establish that visual signs share a universal vocabulary or that apparently received messages have an external source.

## 5. Visionary art: representing the experience of meaning

If the mind can generate the experience of encountering a language, art offers a way to preserve something of that experience. Allyson Grey's *Secret Writing* offers an unusually close parallel. She describes encountering unfamiliar symbols during an LSD experience in 1971, with writing-like forms appearing in the air, around her body, and across surrounding surfaces. Crucially, she describes the resulting symbols as untranslatable and not assigned fixed linguistic meanings [[7]](#ref-7).

That history supplies a concrete example of the path from visionary perception to a reproducible visual alphabet. A human artist can produce forms that convey the appearance and emotional force of language without conveying conventional semantic content. Alex Grey's visionary imagery also explores the relationship between body, consciousness, pattern, and symbolic representation. The resemblance between such works and descriptions of DMT phenomena is worth studying, although it does not imply that every artist or observer experiences identical symbols.

Diana Reed Slattery approached this territory through *xenolinguistics*: the study of linguistic phenomena in psychedelic experience, including apparently alien communications and visual languages. Her 2011 doctoral research and the constructed visual language *Glide* provide a framework for asking how an experience that seems communicative can be documented and compared between individuals [[8]](#ref-8). Together, Grey and Slattery illustrate that a visually coherent, apparently linguistic artifact need not be a translation of an external language.

## 6. Project Serpo: an alleged alien script as a comparison case

This distinction between a visual alphabet and a decipherable language brings us back to the artifact that prompted this inquiry. In March 2006, Project Serpo's anonymous communications included a sheet presented as a sample of the alleged Eben written language. The archive preserves the release, but no authenticated extraterrestrial provenance or independently validated translation accompanies the symbols [[9]](#ref-9). The human-authorship hypothesis is plausible; its truth cannot be decided from apparent visual exoticism alone.

The useful question is not initially, "What does the Serpo language say?" It is, "What features make these marks look like language, and do they behave statistically like reusable signs in a real writing system?" A proper analysis would inventory repeated glyphs, identify recurring components, evaluate complexity and spatial organization, and test possible reading directions. It would also compare the sheet with genuine scripts, human-created ciphers, constructed writing, and asemic art—writing-like marks with no fixed conventional linguistic interpretation.

No exact, independently verified computational decoding of the Serpo sheet is established here. Without a reliable segmentation and transcription, claims about its entropy, grammar, or meaning would be premature. The comparison with psychedelic scripts is therefore a proposed forensic and perceptual research program, not a conclusion that the Serpo sheet arose from DMT.

## 7. The laser experiment: shared code or shared neural processing?

Serpo concerns the provenance of a drawing; the laser experiment adds a different question: what should we infer when multiple observers describe similar experiences? Danny Goler's *Code of Reality* or DMT laser experiment reports that observers looking at a diffracted red laser pattern during DMT experiences describe structured symbols resembling code. Goler's 2025 pilot account was published as a *News and Views* item, and should not be treated as an independently replicated demonstration of symbol-for-symbol agreement [[10]](#ref-10). Claims that different people literally see the same character sequences are therefore stronger than the accessible evidence supports.

The alternative hypothesis is that a common physical stimulus, processed by nervous systems with broadly similar architecture, produces recurring classes of apparent symbols. The result could be scientifically interesting without implying that the laser reveals a hidden programming language embedded in matter. It is vital to separate three outcomes: observers merely say "it looked like writing"; independent drawings show similar visual statistics; or observers identify the *same precise characters in the same locations*, beyond what the physical optics and shared expectations predict. These are different evidentiary thresholds, and only the last would be unusually demanding to explain.

The strongest tests would use independent reports, preregistered scoring criteria, optical control conditions, and safeguards against participants learning what others say they should see. Because lasers and intoxication introduce obvious safety issues, such testing belongs in properly controlled research environments, not as an informal challenge to reproduce the claims.

## 8. Telepathic-seeming transmissions, entities, and instruction

The progression from pattern to apparent meaning does not stop at visible writing. In my experiences, communication has appeared in several forms: internally heard or formulated words, complete conceptual understandings that only later become English, and complex evolving visual structures that seem to communicate through their form. Some episodes produce a sensation of receiving an enormous amount of information or undergoing a change in one's processing, even when little of the content can later be expressed. Others contain highly specific instructions, including messages resembling "We are tuning your brain to receive transmission," requests to study an evolving higher-dimensional form while an apparent intelligence measures the response, or explanations that the perceived world is a simulation designed to illustrate how mind constructs reality.

These messages are best treated as descriptions of what was experienced, not evidence that a sender actually tuned the brain, measured its responses, or supplied information from outside the person. There is nonetheless scientific precedent for studying such encounters seriously: in a survey of 2,561 respondents describing memorable DMT entity encounters, 84% reported communication, while 74% selected an extrasensory or telepathic mode of communication [[11]](#ref-11). Those figures characterize a selected group of reports, not the prevalence of independently existing entities.

One possibility is that altered integration of thought, internal speech, visual imagery, and agency attribution makes self-generated cognitive material feel as though it arrives from elsewhere. Another is that the brain personifies partially inaccessible aspects of its own processing as instructors, examiners, or communicators. These remain hypotheses, not established explanations of each individual report. The distinction between a genuine subjective *experience of transmission* and independently demonstrable *transmission of information* remains crucial.

## 9. The simulation analogy and Dzogchen pointing-out

Some encounters have the quality of being an artificial intelligence inspected by its programmers: an apparent observer is placed within a constructed environment, shown perplexing forms, told to pay attention, and made to feel that its reactions are being examined. This metaphor is illuminating because both artificial and biological systems construct internal models, although their mechanisms are substantially different. The analogy does not establish that human beings are software, that the universe is simulated, or that external programmers exist.

For me, there is a further resonance with Dzogchen pointing-out instruction. I use that comparison to describe the direction of attention in the experience, not to claim that it constitutes a traditional transmission. The apparent message sometimes uses extraordinary perceptual *contents*—glyphs, unfamiliar geometries, convincing environments, or entities—to direct attention toward the conditions in which those contents appear. What interests me is the movement from a particular appearance toward the awareness of that appearance. I would not equate a psychedelic vision with *rigpa*, or take its intensity as evidence of contemplative realization.

The philosophical insight does not require the visionary story to be literally true. A simulated-world narrative could be generated by the mind and still provoke useful reflection about how perception constructs an experienced world. A perceived instructor could be part of the experience and still direct attention toward a valid question. **The usefulness of a lesson and the factual existence of the apparent teacher are separate questions.**

## 10. What the neuroscience establishes—and what it does not

These interpretations must eventually meet evidence beyond introspection. A study using simultaneous EEG and fMRI documented pronounced changes in large-scale brain activity and network organization during acute DMT effects [[12]](#ref-12). Endogenous DMT and biochemical pathways relevant to its synthesis have been investigated in mammals, including measurements in rat brains [[13]](#ref-13). Research has also shown that membrane-permeable psychedelics can activate intracellular 5-HT2A receptors in experimental systems and promote structural plasticity. This is not a simple division in which DMT acts inside neurons and serotonin acts only outside them: access to receptors, transport, and experimental conditions matter. The findings do not establish DMT as a normal human neurotransmitter or show that the feeling of being “retuned” is a direct perception of neural remodeling [[14]](#ref-14).

None of those studies shows that DMT exposes a literal lower-level rendering engine, that repeated trees arise from duplicated neural object templates, or that apparent telepathy constitutes externally transmitted knowledge. What they do show is that DMT engages biological mechanisms capable of substantially changing how experience is organized. The specific pathway from altered brain dynamics to stable skin-bound glyphs or apparent cloned clouds remains to be investigated.

## 11. Predictions and ways to challenge the hypothesis

The broad claim that perception is constructive is not, by itself, a distinctive explanation of these observations. To make the working hypothesis useful, its more specific proposals must risk being wrong. It predicts that the geometry and stability of apparent symbols should depend, at least partly, on the texture and movement of their perceived supports. It predicts that object duplication and spatial tiling can dissociate, because repetition of *identity* is not the same as repetition of *position*. It also predicts that independently drawn psychedelic scripts may share low-level visual statistics with one another and with human writing systems even when they lack consistent syntax or a decodable message.

Those predictions suggest a non-drug research program: compare images of script-like art with genuine scripts and matched random marks; build controlled simulations of repeated objects versus periodic texture; study ordinary perceptual anchoring and visual illusions; and analyze independently gathered phenomenological descriptions without suggesting expected forms in advance. For apparent communications, distinguish retained verifiable information from the intensity of felt understanding. Such methods could refine the hypothesis without requiring people to reproduce risky altered states.

A serious challenge to a wholly internally generated account would be a preregistered, independently replicated case in which observers consistently report new, exact, verifiable information unavailable through ordinary perception, suggestion, shared stimulus properties, or communication between participants. That finding would call for further investigation; it would not, on its own, identify an extraterrestrial sender or prove a simulation. More modestly, if matched comparisons found that psychedelic glyphs were no more alike than ordinary human-made pseudo-writing, we would have to narrow the claim of a distinctive psychedelic visual vocabulary. The account should not absorb every possible result without changing.

## Conclusion: perhaps the code is in the observer

The most interesting feature of these experiences may be not that they reveal another world, but that they expose the constructive character of experiencing this one. Marks become part of a moving hand. Clouds and trees seem to reuse forms while retaining natural positions. Ground textures become tiled environments. Geometric figures become language, language implies meaning, meaning suggests a communicator, and a communicator may supply a story about the nature of reality itself. Each layer can feel immediate and compelling, even when its apparent interpretation is not externally verified.

This perspective neither dismisses the experiences nor treats their narratives as established facts. It asks a deeper question: how does a nervous system generate such coherent, persuasive models, and why do similar categories recur among people who share broadly similar neural architecture? The answer may connect psychedelic phenomenology with the science of vision, the evolution of written symbols, artistic creativity, theories of agency, and contemplative inquiry into awareness.

**Perhaps the mystery is not what the unreadable alien language says. Perhaps it is why the mind can generate something that looks unmistakably like language, feels profoundly meaningful, and yet cannot be read.**
